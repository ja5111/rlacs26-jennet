A heatmap, at first glance, seems to speak for itself. Colors darken, numbers stand out, patterns emerge. When I uploaded a word frequency heatmap to Perplexity AI with no accompanying context, it produced a confident and coherent interpretation almost immediately. It identified the emotional register of the vocabulary, noted that "fear" spiked dramatically in one column, and described "happy" as stable across the texts. It was fluent, organized, and partially wrong in ways that only became visible once context was added.

![The image shared with Perplexity AI]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/heatmap-without-context.png)


## Step 1: What Perplexity Saw Without Context

In my first prompt, I asked Perplexity: *"Explain what you see in this visualization. What does it show? What patterns do you notice?"* Without any information about the texts, Perplexity correctly read the mechanics of the heatmap: rows are words, columns are unnamed categories, color intensity reflects frequency. It identified the most salient pattern accurately — "fear" in the third column stands out as the single darkest cell in the entire grid.

However, it called the five texts "unnamed categories or conditions." That framing is borrowed from experimental or scientific visualization conventions and is technically defensible but can be misleading. A heatmap in a psychology study and a heatmap in a literary corpus look identical. Without knowing which convention applies, the model defaulted to the most generic possible interpretation.

![Perplexity Step 1]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/perplexity-step1.png)

## Step 2: Genre Guessing Under Uncertainty

When I pushed further — *"What genre or type of writing do you think these texts might be?"* — Perplexity offered plausible guesses: romance, young-adult fiction, personal diaries, or social media posts. These are reasonable inferences from emotional vocabulary alone. But it had no way to distinguish between five novels and five Reddit posts. The visualization flattened that difference completely. As Alberto Cairo argues in *How Charts Lie*, visualizations carry implicit rhetorical choices — a raw frequency heatmap implicitly suggests equivalence between its columns, even when the underlying objects are radically different in scale, authorship, and purpose.

![Perplexity Step 2]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/perplexity-step2.png)


## Step 3: Context Sharpens, But Doesn't Contradict

After that, I told Perplexity that the corpus included Rowling's *Deathly Hallows* and *Goblet of Fire*, plus three Harry Potter fan fiction texts, and that the words were chosen to track emotional vocabulary. Its response was notably candid: *"Yes, that context sharpens the interpretation, but it doesn't fundamentally contradict it."*

This is an honest and interesting admission — the model was not entirely wrong before, it was just operating at the wrong level of specificity. The same numbers mean different things in different contexts, and a visualization cannot carry that context on its own.

![Perplexity Step 3]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/perplexity-step3.png)

## Step 4: The Normalization Problem

When I mentioned that the published novels are significantly longer than the fan fiction texts, Perplexity's response was that "raw frequency counts are a noisy and potentially misleading basis for comparison." It explained that a count of 54 in a novel-length text might represent a lower rate of use than a count of 10 in a short fanfic, yet the heatmap visually presents the 54 as the dominant, darkest cell.

The visualization was not lying, exactly, but it was not telling the whole truth either. Normalization — expressing counts as frequency per 1,000 words, for instance — would have been required for genuine comparability. Nothing in the heatmap signals that the columns represent texts of radically different lengths. A reader, human or machine, has no way to know this from the image alone.

![Perplexity Step 4]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/perplexity-step4.png)

## Conclusion

Any attentive reader could describe what they see in a visualization, but description is not interpretation. When the minimum contextual information is absent, even the most careful observer is limited to the surface of the image. Expecting an LLM or a person to reconstruct the analytical intent behind a visualization without that baseline is simply asking too much.

But there is a more interesting problem lurking here, one that applies to human readers more than to LLMs. A reader does not approach a visualization as a blank slate. They come to it through the lens of what they habitually read and work with. A student steeped in psychology research would likely read my heatmap as an experimental design — scanning for group differences rather than literary patterns.

Neither reading is wrong, but both are shaped by habit more than by the data itself. This is what makes visualization both powerful and treacherous. The same image can generate entirely different interpretations depending on who is looking and what they are used to seeing — not because the data is ambiguous, but because the interpretive frame is invisible. Context does not just sharpen interpretation. It determines which interpretation is even possible in the first place.
